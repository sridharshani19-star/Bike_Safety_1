The problem

India loses a life to a road crash
every three minutes

Motorcycles carry the heaviest share of that toll. When a rider goes down alone on a highway, the minutes that follow decide whether they live. Yet the most common outcome is that nobody knows an accident has happened at all.

What actually goes wrong

  • Nobody knows. An unconscious rider cannot call for help, and a crash on an empty stretch can go unnoticed for many minutes.
  • Family cannot find them. Even when informed, relatives rarely know the exact spot on a long highway.
  • Existing systems stop at notification. An alert is sent, and that is the end of it. The information dies there.
  • Nobody moves. A message reaches a group of ten people and every one of them assumes somebody else is already going. This is the bystander effect, and it costs time that the injured rider does not have.
  • Danger is invisible to others. A crash site with an obstruction, spilled oil or emergency vehicles is a hazard to every rider arriving next. No system tells them.
  • Fastest is not safest. Navigation apps optimise for time. None of them know where accidents just happened.
Our approach

Detect. Alert. Locate. Warn.

The device on the bike does more than raise an alarm. Every accident it detects is written into a shared map that other riders read in real time. One rider's crash becomes the next rider's warning.

Detect

Three independent sensors confirm a crash before anything is sent, so a pothole does not trigger a false emergency.

Alert

Family receive the exact coordinates, the time, the impact force and a one-tap navigation link within seconds.

Locate

GPS fixes the spot to within a few metres and ride-booking links are pre-filled for anyone without a vehicle.

Respond

An I proceed button lets one person claim the response, so the group knows who is on the way instead of everyone waiting.

Warn

The location becomes an accident zone. Riders approaching it hear a spoken warning at one kilometre, five hundred and two hundred metres.

Learn

Every incident is stored permanently. Repeated markers in one place reveal a genuinely dangerous stretch of road.

How it works

From impact to warning
in under thirty seconds

Figure 1 — End to end flow from sensor reading to community warning

Step 1 — Three ways to detect a crash

A single sensor produces false alarms. Speed bumps register as impacts. A bike on its side stand registers as a fall. So three independent conditions are watched, and any one of them is enough to start the countdown.

TriggerConditionWhat it catches
G-force> 2.5 G A sudden hard collision
Tilt> 45° held 4 s The bike went down and stayed down
Vibration4 shocks in 400 ms Violent repeated impact
Why the accelerometer learns its own position

At power on, the device averages 120 readings to learn its resting orientation. That means it can be mounted at any angle on any bike, and tilt is measured against that learned reference rather than a fixed assumption.

Step 2 — The twenty second window

A crash is not always an emergency. The buzzer sounds for twenty seconds, beeping faster as time runs out. A conscious rider presses the button once and nothing is sent. This single design decision is what makes the system usable in daily life rather than a source of constant false alarms.

Step 3 — The alert

With no response, the message goes out carrying the exact coordinates, the date and time in twelve hour format, which sensor triggered, the measured impact force, the tilt angle, the satellite count for confidence, a map pin, a one-tap navigation link, and pre-filled booking links for Uber, Ola and Rapido so a relative without a vehicle can reach the spot immediately.

Step 4 — Somebody claims the response

This addresses a specific failure. When an alert reaches ten people, everyone assumes someone else is going. The app shows an I proceed button on every accident alert. The moment one person taps it, every other phone in the group displays their name and the words is taking the necessary steps. Responsibility becomes visible and unambiguous.

Step 5 — The location becomes a warning

The crash site is marked as an accident prone zone for a period the group chooses, anywhere from two minutes to fifteen days, with a radius from ten to five hundred metres. Any rider who comes within that radius gets a spoken warning, a repeating alarm and a red screen. The zone clears itself automatically when it expires.

The novel part

Optimising for safety
instead of speed

Every navigation app in the world answers one question: which road is fastest? This system asks a different one: which road has the fewest recent accidents?

Figure 2 — The app measures every zone's perpendicular distance to each road and selects the route crossing the fewest

When more than one road exists between two places, all of them are fetched. For every active accident zone the app measures its perpendicular distance to each route line, and counts only zones lying within a 350 metre corridor of the road. A crash on a parallel street is correctly ignored. The route with the fewest zones is selected automatically, and the alternatives stay visible so the rider can choose for themselves.

The same calculation gives distance travelled along the route, which is how the app can say forty two kilometres into the journey and count down as you approach.

Two design decisions worth explaining

A GSM module was tested first and removed after it failed to respond to commands at every baud rate, inversion mode and parity setting. WiFi with Telegram replaced it and proved more capable, carrying maps and links that SMS cannot. Separately, a simple tilt switch was replaced by the MPU6050 to obtain an actual angle rather than an on or off state, which is what makes the four second confirmation possible.

Network resilience

The device holds a list of WiFi networks and tries each in turn, rotating between them if a connection drops. Network calls are blocked while the alarm is counting down, because a TLS handshake can stall the processor for several seconds and the cancel button must stay responsive at all times. A memory guard skips a network call rather than freezing when free RAM falls below sixty kilobytes.

Impact

Who this helps

The rider

Help is called even when they cannot call it themselves. A manual SOS is always available by holding the button for eight seconds.

The family

They learn immediately, know exactly where, can navigate straight there, and can see who among them is already going.

Every other rider

They are warned by voice before reaching a fresh crash site, and can choose a road with fewer recent accidents.

Honest limitations

  • No measured false positive rate yet. Thresholds of 2.5 G and 45 degrees are reasoned but not yet validated against road testing data.
  • Requires network coverage. A crash in a dead zone cannot report until connectivity returns.
  • GPS drift. Consumer GPS wanders several metres, so a twenty metre zone boundary is approximate rather than exact.
  • Group scale. The current design suits a family or friend group rather than a city wide deployment, which would need proper authentication.

Stating these plainly is deliberate. A safety system that overstates its reliability is more dangerous than one that is honest about its edges.
